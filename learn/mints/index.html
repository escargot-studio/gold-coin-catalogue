Ancient mints through the ages

What the buildings that made coins looked like, from a Greek city's workroom to Rome's mint and the late Roman mints, and how much of that we really know.

What is left · c. 600 BC – AD 700

No mint survives whole

No ancient mint is still standing, and only a handful have been dug up. What survives is pits, moulds, scraps of metal, blanks that were never struck, and inscriptions naming the staff.

Every Greek mint found so far made bronze small change. The buildings where gold and silver were struck have never been found, so every picture of an ancient mint, including the three here, is a reconstruction. Under each one, plain text marks what the digs show and pink marks our guesses. Mint buildings changed enough over a thousand years that one picture would mislead.

Greek cities · c. 400 – 30 BC

A Greek city mint

At Athens the mint was a plain public building by the market square, about 27 by 29 metres. Its big workroom had pits, water basins and a drain, and bronze blanks still lay on the floor.

The blanks were made by forging a bronze rod, scoring it with a chisel, chopping off a slice and hammering it flat. The same chopped blanks turn up at Halieis and Olynthos in the 4th century BC. The first diggers took the pits for furnaces, but a lab test in 1997 found no trace of melting: the metal arrived ready. At Halieis the mint stood against the city wall, and its excavators thought coins were struck there 'only as required'. A Greek mint was a part-time workshop, not a factory.

The pits held clay basins scorched by fire. What was heated in them is not known.

Sources
  • Camp and Kroll, The Agora Mint and Athenian Bronze Coinage (Hesperia, free)

Greek cities · c. 407 – 375 BC

Where was the silver struck?

Not in that building: tests there found 'only the slightest traces of silver'. Ancient writers name a separate silver mint at Athens, the argyrokopeion. It has never been found.

When Athens ran short of silver near the end of its war with Sparta, it melted gold statues of Victory on the Acropolis and struck gold coins, and afterwards the dies were dedicated to the goddess there. A law of 375 BC set a public slave, the tester, among the bankers' tables in the market to check coins: fakes were cut through and given to the Mother of the Gods.

Rome · c. AD 80 – 270

Rome's mint

After a fire in AD 80, Rome's mint moved near the Colosseum, probably into a plain block of small vaulted rooms around a courtyard, now under the church of San Clemente. In AD 115 its staff set up statues naming about ninety of themselves.

Before that the mint stood beside the temple of Juno Moneta on the Capitol, 'where the temple and mint of Moneta now stand', wrote the Roman historian Livy. The AD 115 list names Felix, a freedman of the emperor, who checked the gold, silver and bronze; 16 workshop heads; and a striking crew of 17 signatores, 11 suppostores who set the blanks and 32 malliatores who swung the hammers. Half were slaves. Coins were counted so many to the pound. In AD 271 the mint workers rose against the emperor Aurelian.

Whether the building under San Clemente was the mint is disputed. What a signator did is too.

Late Rome · c. AD 300 – 450

Blanks by the hundred

Late Roman mints cast their bronze blanks in throwaway clay moulds, up to 192 at a pour. At Thessaloniki the mint sat in rooms at the end of the city forum, with four kilns.

Each mould was six thin clay panels with two rows of eight hollows on each face, wrapped in raw clay and fired. Bronze poured in through a narrow channel filled every hollow, and the mould was broken to get the blanks out. One broken piece still holds four blanks. From about AD 294 some fifteen imperial mints each ran several teams, and each team put its own number on the coins, so bad work could be traced.

Sources
  • Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki: a clay mould for bronze blanks
Late Rome · AD 367 – 450

Gold followed the emperor

Gold was different. From about AD 367 tax gold was melted into bars and sent to the emperor's treasury, and gold coins were struck where the court was.

A hoard of fifteen such bars was found in a Carpathian mountain pass. They were stamped at Sirmium about AD 379–380, signed by the assayers Lucianus and Flavius Flavianus, and are now in London and Vienna. Solidi marked COMOB under the design are usually read as 'refined gold of the court'. The court's own gold workshop has never been found.

How literally the gold mint travelled with the emperor is debated.
