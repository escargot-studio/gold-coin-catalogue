Coin names and sizes

Stater, drachm, aureus, solidus: what the names meant, how big the coins were, and how they changed over 1,800 years.

All the main gold coins, to scale

Each disc's area matches the coin's weight. Gold coins got smaller over the centuries, apart from a few giant showpieces.

The faint dashed circle at the bottom is a modern 1 oz gold coin (31.1 g), for scale. Weights are typical full-weight figures; real coins vary a little. Electrum is a natural gold-silver mix. Gold names count in two ways. Some count gold: a distater is two staters, Carthage's trihemistater one and a half, the Ptolemies' pentadrachm five gold drachms. Others count the silver the coin was worth: the mnaieion was worth a mina (100 silver drachms) and Syracuse's 100 litrai coin 100 silver litrai. Five litrai made one silver drachm, so auction catalogues list Syracuse's 2.9 g gold as a 'decadrachm or 50 litrae' and its 1.15 g gold as a 'tetradrachm or 20 litrae'. Carthage's later staters and trihemistaters are electrum, so they weigh less than the gold ones (about 7.5 g and 11 g). Doubles such as the distater and the double daric were struck in far smaller numbers than single staters, so today they cost many times more than two singles.

Sources
  • Kraay, Archaic and Classical Greek Coins (1976)
  • Grierson, Byzantine Coins (1982)
  • Codex Theodosianus 12.6.13

Stater: 'the weigher'

The first coins, struck in Lydia around 600 BC, were electrum staters of about 14 g, cut down into thirds, sixths, twelfths and even smaller pieces.

Stater just means a standard weight. A sixth was a hekte: cities such as Mytilene and Phocaea struck thousands of these thumbnail-sized electrum coins for trade around the Aegean.

Sources
  • Kraay, Archaic and Classical Greek Coins (1976)
  • Berlin Münzkabinett 18200094, 18299995, 18202884

The gold stater and its halves

Philip II and Alexander struck pure gold staters of about 8.6 g, with doubles, halves, quarters and eighths. One gold stater was worth 20 silver drachms.

Sicilian cities struck small gold too: a quarter stater of Akragas shows a crab, the city's badge.

Sources
  • Price, The Coinage in the Name of Alexander the Great and Philip Arrhidaeus (1991)
  • BnF, Cabinet des médailles

Silver names: obol, drachm, tetradrachm

Everyday Greek money was silver. An obol was a 'spit' of iron, a drachm a 'handful' of six obols, and a tetradrachm four drachms (about 17 g).

Athens' owl tetradrachm was the dollar of the Greek world. Syracuse went further with the decadrachm, ten drachms in one coin, made for victory games.

Sources
  • Plutarch, Greek biographer (about AD 100), Life of Lysander 17: iron spits as money

Sicily counted in litrai

Syracuse marked its gold in litrai, a local silver unit of a fifth of a drachm. A gold 100-litrai piece was worth two silver decadrachms.

Dionysios I struck these during his wars with Carthage, with Herakles wrestling the Nemean lion on the back.

Sources
  • Kraay, Archaic and Classical Greek Coins (1976)
  • Berlin Münzkabinett 18205549

Giant gold of the kings

After Alexander, the Ptolemies of Egypt struck gold 'mnaieia' of about 28 g, each worth a mina (100 silver drachms), and half pieces worth 50.

The biggest ancient gold coin of all is the 20-stater piece of Eukratides of Bactria, 169 g, now in Paris. It was a showpiece, not money for the market.

Sources
  • Berlin Münzkabinett 18200181
  • BnF, Cabinet des médailles E3605

Rome's first gold: marked in asses

During the war with Hannibal, around 211 BC, Rome struck emergency gold marked with its value in bronze asses: LX (60), XL (40) and XX (20).

Before that Rome hardly used gold at all. The marks told people how much bronze money each little piece stood for.

Sources
  • Crawford, Roman Republican Coinage 44/2
  • Berlin Münzkabinett 18200936

The aureus and its family

Under Augustus: 1 gold aureus = 25 silver denarii = 100 brass sestertii = 400 bronze asses. Half an aureus was a gold quinarius.

In the troubled third century emperors also struck double aurei (binios), the emperor often wearing a radiate crown to mark the double value.

Sources
  • Cassius Dio, Roman senator and historian (about AD 200), 55.12.4: the aureus worth 100 sesterces

Solidus, semissis, tremissis

Constantine's solidus (4.5 g, 72 to the Roman pound) came with a half (semissis) and a third (tremissis). The tremissis became the gold coin of the Franks and Visigoths.

A solidus was reckoned at 24 siliquae, in Greek keratia (carob seeds): the root of our 24-carat scale. Our words 'soldier' and the old 's' of shillings both go back to the solidus.

Sources
  • Codex Theodosianus 12.6.13
  • Grierson, Byzantine Coins (1982)
  • Berlin Münzkabinett 18210969, 18222073

Gifts of several solidi

Emperors handed out gold medallions worth several solidi, 1½, 4½ or even 9, to officers and allies on great occasions.

Many have been found far beyond the empire's borders, in the treasure of barbarian chiefs, often with a loop for wearing.

Sources
  • Berlin Münzkabinett 18200863

Byzantine names: nomisma to hyperpyron

In Greek the solidus was the nomisma. In the 960s a lighter tetarteron appeared beside the full-weight histamenon; by the 1080s the gold was badly debased.

Alexios I's reform of 1092 replaced it all with the hyperpyron ('super-refined'), about 20½ carats fine, the empire's gold coin until the 14th century.

Sources
  • Grierson, Byzantine Coins (1982)
  • Cleveland Museum of Art 1968.60, 2001.25
