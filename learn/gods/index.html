How to spot a god

Ancient artists gave every god a few fixed clues, like a uniform. Learn the clues and you can name the figure on almost any coin.

Tell me more

Faces help less than you would think: engravers drew the gods to an ideal and copied one another for centuries. What they wear and carry does. The same goddess can also have two names, a Greek one and a Roman one, because the Romans matched their own gods to the Greek ones. Roman coins added a new crowd: ideas such as Peace and Good Luck, each shown as a woman holding her own clue.

The gold coins on the cards that follow: Alexander's stater (Athena, Nike), Maximian (Hercules), Nero (Jupiter), Augustus (Apollo), Trajan (Fortuna) and Vespasian (Pax). Photos: Münzkabinett Berlin, Public Domain Mark.

Zeus: the king of the gods

A bearded god on a throne with a sceptre, or hurling a thunderbolt, is Zeus, Jupiter to the Romans: the ruler of the gods, and so the favourite partner of human rulers.

Picture: Münzkabinett, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin · Public Domain Mark 1.0
Tell me more

His clues are the thunderbolt, the sceptre and the eagle. On Alexander's silver he sits on the back, an eagle on his hand. Nero's aureus calls him IVPPITER CVSTOS, 'Jupiter the guardian'. Three centuries later Diocletian went further: he took the name Iovius, Jupiter's man, while his partner Maximian was Herculius. On this aureus Jupiter strikes down a Giant with his thunderbolt, under IOVI FVLGERATORI, 'to Jupiter the lightning-thrower'. The Christian writer Lactantius later sneered at those 'surnames of the Jovii and the Herculii, once so glorious'.

Jupiter enthroned with sceptre and thunderbolt on an aureus of Nero (AD 54–68), and Jupiter striking a Giant on an aureus of Diocletian (AD 284–305).

Athena: the face of trusted money

The goddess in the helmet is Athena, Minerva to the Romans. She was the face of Athens's trusted owls, then of Alexander's gold, then of Rome's.

Picture: Bibliothèque nationale de France, via Wikimedia Commons · Public domain; Münzkabinett, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin · Public Domain Mark 1.0
Tell me more

Athens put her head on its silver owls for about two centuries and hardly changed the design, because traders all round the Mediterranean trusted the old look. When the city struck gold in 407/6 BC, melted from the golden statues of Victory on the Acropolis, it kept the same Athena and owl; soon after, the comic playwright Aristophanes compared Athens's citizens to 'the old coinage and the new gold' in his Frogs. Alexander put her on his gold stater in a Corinthian helmet, with Victory on the back: a goddess of wisdom and war for a king at war. His generals and later cities kept striking that design for generations. In Rome she became Minerva, and Domitian, who the biographer Suetonius says worshipped her with superstitious veneration, put her on coin after coin.

Athena on gold: Athens, stater of 407–404 BC; Alexander the Great, stater, about 323–316 BC; and as Minerva, with shield, spear and owl, on an aureus of Domitian, AD 88.

Apollo: the god with the laurel

A young god with a laurel wreath in his hair is Apollo, god of music, prophecy and light. Philip II of Macedon put him on the gold that made his kingdom rich.

Picture: Münzkabinett, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin · Public Domain Mark 1.0
Tell me more

Philip's gold came from the mines he took near Mount Pangaion; the historian Diodorus says his gold coins were called Philippeioi after him, and raised Macedon to a great power. Apollo's head stayed on them for decades after Philip's death. Augustus owed Apollo something too: he beat Antony and Cleopatra in 31 BC beside Apollo's temple at Actium, and Suetonius says he enlarged that temple afterwards. On this aureus Apollo of Actium stands with his lyre, above the word ACT.

Apollo with his laurel wreath on a gold stater of Philip II (359–336 BC), and Apollo of Actium with his lyre on an aureus of Augustus.

Herakles: the king's ancestor

The young man wearing a lion's scalp is Herakles, Hercules to the Romans. Macedon's kings counted him as their ancestor, so Alexander put him on his silver.

Picture: Münzkabinett, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin · Public Domain Mark 1.0
Tell me more

Herakles killed the Nemean lion and wore its skin, so the lion's head over his own is how you know him; his club and bow are the other clues. The Athenian orator Isocrates reminded Alexander's father Philip that Thebes honoured 'the founder of your race', meaning Herakles. Alexander's silver showed Herakles on the front and Zeus enthroned on the back, while his gold carried Athena, and his successors and later cities went on striking his silver for up to two centuries. Rome's emperors borrowed the hero too. The historian Cassius Dio says Commodus called himself 'the Roman Hercules' and had statues made of himself dressed as Hercules. Maximian, Diocletian's co-emperor, took Hercules as his protector and the name Herculius: on this aureus of AD 293 he wears the lion's skin himself, and on the back Hercules fights the Hydra under HERCVLI DEBELLAT, 'to Hercules the conqueror'.

Herakles in his lion skin on a silver tetradrachm of Alexander, about 323–320 BC (silver, because Alexander's Herakles coins were his silver ones). Maximian wearing the lion skin, and Hercules fighting the Hydra, on a gold aureus of AD 293.

Nike: Victory with wings

A winged woman holding a wreath is Nike, Victory to the Romans. Rulers loved her, and she stayed on gold even after the empire became Christian.

Picture: Münzkabinett, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin · Public Domain Mark 1.0
Tell me more

On Alexander's gold stater she stands on the back, holding out a victor's wreath. Roman emperors showed her crowning them, carrying trophies, or driving a four-horse chariot as on this aureus of Antoninus Pius, VICTORIA AVG, 'the emperor's victory'. When the empire became Christian she kept her place: on the gold of Anastasius, about AD 500, she holds a tall cross, and on later Byzantine gold she turns into an angel.

Victory on gold for 800 years: Alexander's stater, about 323–316 BC; Antoninus Pius's aureus, AD 138–161; and a solidus of Anastasius, AD 491–518, where she holds a cross.

Source: Münzkabinett Berlin 18216073: gold stater of Alexander III, Nike ↗ · Münzkabinett Berlin 18273156: aureus of Antoninus Pius, Victory in a quadriga ↗ · Münzkabinett Berlin 18245255: solidus of Anastasius I, Victory with a cross ↗

Rome's ideas as goddesses

Many women on Roman gold are not goddesses from myths but ideas: Peace, Good Luck, Concord, and Roma, the city herself. Each one carries her own clue.

Picture: Münzkabinett, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin · Public Domain Mark 1.0
Tell me more

Roma wears a helmet like Athena's and sits on shields; on this aureus of Titus the she-wolf with Romulus and Remus lies at her feet. Fortuna holds a ship's rudder, because she steers what happens, and a horn of plenty. Pax holds an olive branch. These figures turned a coin into a message: after the civil war of AD 69 Vespasian struck Pax again and again, and Suetonius says he built a temple of Peace by the Forum. When you see a woman on a Roman coin, read the word around her: it usually names her.

Roma on an aureus of Titus, about AD 77, Fortuna on an aureus of Trajan (AD 98–117), and Pax on an aureus of Vespasian (AD 69–79).
